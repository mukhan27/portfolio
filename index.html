ES 55 · Design & Manufacturing/ Reverse engineering/ With Davu

Costing a World Cup Tumbler From the Factory Floor Up

A licensed FIFA 2026 “Rowdy Cup” — a 700 mL stainless tumbler with a whistle built into its base — taken apart, modelled, and costed part by part.

Teardown and reconstruction

We disassembled the cup down to eighteen line items: a 0.4 mm formed stainless sleeve, a food-grade PP inner vessel with an integral straw channel, a bonded bottom hub carrying the sound hole and the whistle threads, a slotted base cap, a 3.3 g silicone acoustic diaphragm, an SAN lid with a slider, two gaskets, an extruded straw, a heat-transfer graphic, and the packaging.

We had no scale. So the measuring instrument became a SolidWorks model — rebuilding each part from calipers and photographs, then reading volumes off the CAD and multiplying by material density to get masses. Those masses, against 2026 resin, silicone, steel and corrugated prices, gave the material column of the bill of materials.

Process sheet and labour

Twenty-four steps, from stamping the steel blank through roll-forming, seam welding, tapering, coating, heat-transfer graphics, six injection-moulding operations, assembly and packing. Each step was assigned a labour time and burdened at a real $4.50/hr Chinese rate, then re-run at a hypothetical $46/hr US rate to see what the rate alone was worth.

What the numbers said

  • LBM came to $1.73 per unit at the China rate — $1.35 material, $0.38 labour and burden. The identical process sheet costs $5.19 at the US rate.
  • Labour rate outweighs the bill of materials. Labour is 22% of the real total and 74% of the hypothetical one. Which country builds it matters more than any single part on the BOM.
  • The FIFA royalty (~$1.80/unit) costs more than every material combined ($1.35). On licensed merchandise, the licence is the expensive component.
  • Our first-pass estimate of $8.00 was wrong in an instructive way. We had reasoned it backwards from a $12.97 clearance price, which lands near the licensee’s landed cost, not the factory’s manufacturing cost. The gap between them is tooling amortisation, overhead, freight and the royalty.

What the parts admitted

The base cap’s moulded text reads ZHC Corp. Canada and 23 oz; the carton says Rowdy Cup and 24 oz. This is a licensed graphic wrap on an existing product, not a clean-sheet design. Elsewhere, squeezed-out adhesive visible at the bottom joint told us the green shell is glued to the black hub rather than moulded as one piece or snap-fit — a detail that changed both the process sheet and one of our DFM recommendations.

Design for manufacturing

  • Specify pre-coated steel coil and delete the clean–coat–cure sequence entirely.
  • Family-tool the four small plastic parts (slider, base cap, diaphragm, gasket). No unit-cost change, but roughly $20–30k less tooling capital — worth a great deal on a product with one tournament of shelf life.
  • Crimp the hub into the shell instead of bonding it, removing the adhesive line and its cure time.
Method note We used Claude to read the fine moulded text off the teardown photographs and to research current resin, silicone and stainless pricing. The disassembly, the CAD, the process sheet and every costing judgement are ours.

ES 120 · Mechanics of Solids/ Finite element analysis/ COMSOL

Finding Where Two Textbook Assumptions Stop Being True

ES 120 hands you a linear theory and a promise that σ = P/A works “far enough” from the load. I built the simulations to find the edges of both claims and put numbers on them.

Setup

A standard ASTM E8 round tensile specimen — 145 mm long, 50 mm gauge, 12.5 mm gauge diameter, 20 mm grips, 10 mm fillets — in A36 steel, fixed at the lower face and loaded axially on a tetrahedral mesh.

Before trusting anything, I checked the model against an ES 120 hand calculation at 200 MPa: both gave 512 MPa in the gauge. Tip displacement came to 0.232 mm by hand against 0.250 mm in COMSOL — the difference is fillet compliance, which the three-cylinder formula cannot see. A five-level mesh study from ~1,500 to ~41,000 elements moved the answer by 0.084%, so the discrepancy was physics, not discretisation.

Assumption I — linearising elasticity

I swept applied stress across eight log-spaced values, from ε = 10⁻⁴ to 0.3, running each one with geometric nonlinearity on and off, and compared tip displacement.

  • Linear theory tracks the full nonlinear result to within 1% up to ε ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻³, and within 5% to ε ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻².
  • Steel structures are designed below ε ≈ 10⁻³ to stay clear of yield, so the linear theory carries roughly an order of magnitude of strain margin in any realistic application.
  • It only fails meaningfully past a few percent strain — well beyond where the steel itself stopped behaving elastically. At ε = 0.1 the two disagree by over 300%; at ε = 0.3 the nonlinear solver finds no stable equilibrium at all.

Assumption II — Saint-Venant and idealised stress

Three statically equivalent loadings, each delivering the same 62.83 kN to the end face: a uniform traction (the textbook idealisation), a centred point load at a single vertex, and an outer-ring traction standing in for grip jaws. On nine cross-sections along the bar I measured a dimensionless non-uniformity, η = σstd/|σavg|, which is zero for a perfectly uniform distribution.

  • Point and ring loads start at η ≈ 21% at the loaded face and decay to a few percent within about one grip diameter. The uniform load sits near the ~4% noise floor throughout.
  • By the gauge centre the three are essentially indistinguishable. That is Saint-Venant’s principle, measured rather than asserted — and it is why σ = P/A is legitimate in the gauge of a real tensile test with real grips.
  • The caveat I did not expect: the cross-section inside the fillet shows η ≈ 33% for all three loadings. Because it is identical regardless of how the bar is loaded, it is not a Saint-Venant effect at all — it is an ordinary stress concentration at a change in section. I excluded those stations from the decay analysis; they need a stress-concentration-factor treatment instead.

Conclusion

Both assumptions hold comfortably in the regime where ES 120 actually applies them. The full nonlinear three-dimensional theory adds nothing of practical value to a steel tensile specimen — except as a way of locating exactly where the simple theory gives out: at fillets, near loads, and past the elastic limit.

IgNYte Lab · NYU/ Enclosure design/ Embedded telemetry

A Sensor Built To Be Left On A Roof

Street-level monitors miss what the air does at rooftop height — where buildings draw their intake. I worked on the enclosure and the data path for a low-cost NO₂, O₃ and PM sensor designed to be deployed across New York City rooftops and then left alone.

The gap

NYC air quality is well instrumented at street level and barely instrumented at the altitude where air handling units actually sit. PM2.5 in the city has reached 117 µg/m³ during wildfire smoke events — eight times the WHO guideline — and rooftop behaviour of NO₂, O₃ and particulate matter is largely unmeasured. The lab’s aim is to fill that gap with sensors cheap enough to deploy in quantity.

Learning from what already exists

I surveyed four commercial monitors — IQAir, Aeroqual, the Air Quality Egg and Airly — for what to borrow and what to avoid. The recurring good ideas were open bottoms for passive airflow and a roof for weather protection. The recurring problems were disqualifying for our use: mains power, housings sized for far more sensors than we needed, and mounts with height limits.

Enclosure

An open-bottom housing with a pitched roof carrying a solar panel, a nylon outer shell over a cheaper plastic interior, a dedicated shelf for the battery, a separate NO₂/O₃ sensor block hanging below the main body where it sees outside air directly, and a pipe-clamp mount that grips railings and stanchions rather than needing a prepared surface.

Fixing the data path

The original telemetry chain was LoRa: sensor → gateway → database. It kept failing, on range and on gateway faults, and a rooftop is the worst possible place to debug an intermediate box. We replaced it with a SIM7028 cellular module writing directly to Firebase — sensor → SIM → Firebase. One fewer thing on the roof that can go wrong.

Next

Manufacture the PCB, deploy a unit on a roof, and revise from what the deployment breaks — a 3D-printed attachment system with cable ties spaced further from centre, a modified sensor block that drops the front screw, and screw protection at the rear.

ES 50 · Electrical Engineering/ Mechatronics/ With Federico Garcia Alzate

A Checkerboard That Knows Where Its Pieces Are

Thirty-two Hall effect sensors under the dark squares, eight shift registers to get them into an Arduino that does not have thirty-two spare pins, and a 16×16 LED matrix mirroring the board in real time.

Sensing

A DRV5032DU digital Hall sensor sits under each of the 32 dark squares, soldered to insulated leads and fixed to the underside of the board. Each checker carries a neodymium disc in its base; when a piece lands, the sensor below it goes LOW. Presence becomes a clean binary signal with no thresholding to tune.

Getting 32 inputs into an Arduino Uno

Eight SN74HC165N parallel-in / serial-out shift registers, one per rank, four active bits each. We deliberately did not daisy-chain them: every register’s QH goes to its own digital pin, 13 down to 6, off a shared clock and latch. Chaining would have saved pins, but keeping the ranks independent meant a misbehaving row could be isolated and read on its own — which, with 32 hand-soldered sensors under a board, was worth every pin it cost.

Mapping board to matrix

The matrix is wired serpentine, and its origin sits at the bottom-left of our board rather than the top-left of the coordinate system. So a shiftToXY[8][8][2] lookup carries every (rank, bit) pair to its LED coordinates, with white squares marked {-1,-1} and skipped, and an XY() function folds that into the right NeoPixel index. Each square renders as a 2×2 block: green when occupied, yellow when empty.

Structure

Four corner legs lift the board, two side legs tie its halves together, and a platform hides the wiring while holding the LED matrix in register beneath the squares it represents — all designed in SolidWorks and printed in PLA. Assembled, none of the electronics are visible.

What we did not finish, and why

  • Move suggestion. Two buttons were wired and the software was scaffolded to scan the board array for legal diagonals and light the targets in a third colour. The logic did not land before the deadline. We chose stable real-time tracking over a half-working feature.
  • Digital sensors can only answer “magnet or not.” They cannot distinguish red from black, or a king from a man. Analog Hall sensors with graded magnet strengths would fix both at once — the single change that would most improve a second version.
  • Power. Two 4.5 V packs through an SPDT switch, with brightness capped at 10/255. A 256-pixel WS2812B matrix at full brightness needs a real 5 V supply, not batteries in parallel.

ES 51 · Computer-Aided Machine Design/ Machining & CAD/ Team 3

The Wandering Walruses

A five-pound competition robot built from stock inside an eleven-inch cube: turned shafts, a milled wax mould, cast silicone wheels, a belt-lifted arm and a printed claw. It scored eleven points and never made the ramp it was built for.

The brief

Collect hockey pucks, dog toys and ping-pong balls; deposit them in raised bins; climb a 15° ramp; pass under an 8.5-inch bridge; and fit inside the 11×11×11-inch “Box of Justice” at impound. Materials were capped at three sheets of ¼-inch acrylic and one of ⅛-inch.

Choosing a concept

Four concepts went through cardboard prototypes and a Pugh matrix. A drivetrain-only robot for the 30° centre ramp would have tested nothing. A forklift needed a vertical support taller than the bridge clearance would allow. Treads were disproportionate effort for one-point ping-pong balls. Arm and claw won, and the cardboard mock-up immediately showed we needed a cutout in the front of the baseplate for the arm to reach the floor.

Building it

  • I built a gearbox and motor configuration as an individual project; two of those went into the final drivetrain.
  • Shafts turned on the lathe; the 1×1-inch aluminium arm tower cut on the horizontal bandsaw and drill-pressed for bushings; base and side plates laser-cut and joined with T-slots.
  • The arm rides a 15-inch belt over a 64-tooth printed pulley. SolidWorks placed the tower and motor so the belt length came out exactly right — which made it a fight to fit, and made the tensioner we had designed unnecessary.
  • Printed PLA claw with 32-tooth acetal gears and a servo. PLA alone slipped on the pucks, so we milled a wax mould on the CNC and cast Ecoflex-50 silicone inserts to raise the friction. The four drive wheels are cast the same way.
  • Roughly a dozen cardboard baseplate iterations before the acrylic ones, because out-of-tolerance gearbox parts kept refusing to match the CAD.

Calculations, and the one that was wrong

Centre of mass was placed 4.36 inches from the rear wheel contact point, from d = xcmcos θ − ycmsin θ, and the gearboxes, arm and motors were positioned to put it there. Static tests found how high the claw could be carried before the robot would tip. Wheel torque came out to 3.43 lb-in at 30°.

In competition the robot could not climb the 30° centre ramp — the highest-value obstacle on the field. It was not a balance problem. The torque calculation said two-wheel drive had enough, and the calculation was wrong because it assumed the wheels gripped. On turf they slipped. Late in the build we had also dropped the belts that would have made it pseudo-four-wheel drive, because under time pressure they would not stretch onto the front pulleys.

Thirty minutes before impound We left the arm raised while driving a ramp, the robot tipped forward, and the weight snapped the ⅛-inch acrylic arm bars. We laser-cut replacements, stripped the arm and rebuilt it in time — and spent the hour we would otherwise have used fixing wheel tension. ¼-inch acrylic would likely have survived it, and that one material choice probably cost us the ramp.

Result

Seven points in each of the first two rounds. Eleven in the third — two hockey pucks, two ping-pong balls, and a pass under the bridge.
